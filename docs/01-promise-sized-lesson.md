# Pick One Promise-Sized Lesson

Your book contains many helpful things. That is now a problem, because a
course can only carry one promise at a time. A mini-course that promises
"everything in my book" is a bundle, not a course — and bundles get browsed,
while lessons get finished.

## What "promise-sized" means

A promise-sized lesson makes one specific claim to one specific person and
keeps it:

> After this lesson, you will be able to ______, on your own, in about
> ______ minutes.

Fill both blanks with real words. If the first blank is vague ("understand
AI better") or the second blank is huge ("in a few weeks"), the lesson is
either too fuzzy or too large.

**Kitchen test:** if a friend repeated the promise back to you at dinner and
someone else asked "what do you get?", could they answer it in one sentence?
"Learn AI" fails. "Set up a private AI chat on your own computer that works
without internet" passes.

## How to extract the lesson from your book

Your book is a warehouse of promises. Go shopping with a sentence, not a
cart.

1. **List the end-states your book enables.** Not chapters — outcomes. "You
   will be able to run a free AI coding tool on a ten-year-old laptop" is an
   end-state. Chapter 4 is not.
2. **Mark the ones readers actually finish.** Look at which sections people
   bookmark, screenshot, or email you about. Those are load-bearing walls.
3. **Pick the smallest complete one.** A mini-course lives or dies on
   *completion*. A reader who finishes a small lesson trusts you for the
   next one. A reader who stalls on a grand one bounces off the offer.
4. **Name it as a door, not a shelf.** Title the lesson after the door the
   reader walks through: "Draft Your First Lesson Page in One Sitting" beats
   "Course Building Sub-topics."

### Worked example

From a hypothetical book *The Massive AI Manual*, three candidate promises:

| Candidate | Reader | Finish line | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Understand all of modern AI | everyone | nothing checkable | Too big. Not a lesson. |
| Set up a local AI chat on your own computer | beginners with a laptop | chat works offline in ~40 min | One promise, one person, one finish. Pick this. |
| Build an agent that books appointments | indie business owners | working agent, ~3 evenings | Great, but it is lesson #2. Keep it warm. |

## Three litmus tests before you commit

- **The copy-paste test:** can you lift 80% of the raw material straight
  from your book? If you must invent new material, it is the wrong slice.
- **The 90-minute test:** does the recorded content fit comfortably inside
  60–90 minutes of video total, once the exercises are separate? Longer
  belongs in a different format.
- **The printable test:** can you name one printable (a checklist, a
  worksheet, a cheat sheet) that makes the lesson work *on paper*? If no
  printable suggests itself, the promise may need to be smaller.

## Anti-patterns (said with love)

- Picking the lesson that impresses peers instead of the one a beginner can
  finish at 9 p.m. after work.
- Turning one lesson into seven modules by committee-in-your-head. Resist.
  Seven modules is a real course, and the real course is not this project.
- Choosing a promise that requires your *personal* presence (live calls) to
  keep. The promise must be keepable from files and pages.

## Exercise (5 minutes, on paper)

1. Write five end-states your book enables, one per line, as "After this you
   can ______."
2. Star the one you would pick if you had *one week* to ship.
3. Fill in the sentence: "After this lesson, my reader — ____, a ____ who
   fears ____ — will be able to ____, on their own, in about ____ minutes."
4. Keep the sheet. The outline in the next chapter eats it.
