# Outline at a Reader-Finishable Pace

Courses usually die in the middle. Not at purchase, not at lesson one — in
the middle, where a well-meaning author stacked forty minutes of material on
top of a reader's Tuesday. This chapter is about pacing the outline so a
normal person with a job, a phone, and finite attention actually finishes.

## Start from time, not from topics

Pick the total budget first. For a mini-course born from a book chapter:

- **Soft total:** 60–90 minutes of taught material, all videos combined.
- **Sessions:** split it so no single sitting exceeds ~25 minutes of
  watching *plus* 10 minutes of doing.

A tired reader can commit to 25 minutes. Nobody can commit to "watch it all
tonight."

### The arithmetic of finishability

Use ugly, honest numbers:

- An average watch speed runs about 1.5 minutes of video per slide/beat.
- A reader at 1.25× playback finishes faster, but pace the outline assuming
  1× — the promise is "you can finish," not "power users can speedrun."
- For a 90-minute total, that is roughly **4 short videos** of 18–22 minutes,
  or **6 short ones** of 12–15 minutes. Six shorter beats four longer,
  because restart cost is real.

## The four-part shape (use it straight)

| Part | Purpose | Share of time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hook (2–3 min) | Restate the promise, show the finish line, prove it is small. | ~10% |
| Foundation (15–20 min) | The one concept everything else stands on. | ~35% |
| Build-along (30–45 min) | Do the thing together, in small named steps. | ~45% |
| Wrap (3–5 min) | Confirm the finish line was crossed; point at the printable. | ~10% |

Round each named step of the build-along to a multiple of five minutes. If a
step would take twelve minutes, either split it into 10 and leave the last
two as an exercise, or trim it to ten. Odd lengths feel long; round ones
feel finished.

## Cut with two knives

1. **The beginner knife:** the outline step needs no prerequisites beyond
   what lesson zero covered. Anything requiring "first make sure you
   already know X" is either moved into the foundation, or cut.
2. **The pride knife:** cut the parts that exist to show how much you know.
   A mini-course promises a finish line, not a tour of your expertise. Put
   the showpieces in your book — that is what the book is for.

## Name stopping points out loud

At the end of every video, say the exact restart command the viewer needs,
with a file, a verb, and a screen to return to: "Next time, open
`03-build.mp4`, run the setup you saved, and pick up at step 3.2." Restart
cost is the killer; you kill it by scripting the bridge between sittings.

## Exercise (10 minutes)

1. Write your promise sentence from the last chapter at the top of a page.
2. Draft the outline as a table: `Step | Minutes | Visible result`.
3. Every step's "Visible result" must be something the reader can see,
   hear, or run — not "understand" or "learn about."
4. Add up minutes. If you exceeded 90, apply the beginner knife before the
   pride knife, then recheck.
5. Mark where each sitting ends and write its one-sentence restart command.

Keep this table. The next chapter records it one video at a time, and the
lesson template turns it into a page.
